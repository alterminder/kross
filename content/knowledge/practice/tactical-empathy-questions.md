# Tactical Empathy and Calibrated Questions (Voss)

## What it is for

*Never Split the Difference* adapts crisis and hostage-negotiation techniques to business and everyday negotiation. Voss argues that emotion drives negotiation more than logic, and uses "tactical empathy": recognising and voicing the other side's feelings and view without agreeing with them, to lower defences and get them talking. This note shows how to use these techniques consistently with CCHN.

## The method

The book's main listening and questioning tools:

- **Mirroring.** Repeat the last few key words the counterpart said, as a light question, then wait. It invites them to say more.
- **Labelling.** Name the emotion or concern you perceive: "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…". Then stop talking. Voss prefers phrasing that does not start with "I".
- **Effective pauses.** Silence after a label or a mirror gives the counterpart room to fill it, often with useful information.
- **Calibrated questions.** Open questions starting with "how" or "what", such as "How am I supposed to do that?" or "What is most important to you here?". They invite the counterpart to work on your problem. Voss advises avoiding "why", which can sound accusatory.
- **Accusation audit.** Before making a request, name the negative things the counterpart may already think about you, to take the sting out of them.
- **Summaries that earn "That's right".** Restate the counterpart's view so well that they confirm it.

## At the table

In a humanitarian negotiation, use the listening tools in the order CCHN gives for de-escalation:
1. Pause (CCHN says up to 7 seconds, depending on culture).
2. Acknowledge the emotion in words.
3. Reformulate the issue and check it.
4. Ask open, then closed, then open questions to let the counterpart propose options.

Example lines:
- "I hear you." [pause] "You have been waiting three days for an answer from us. Am I right?"
- "I can see you are worried about who is on those trucks."
- "How would you suggest we deal with the risk on that road?" → "Are there any other possibilities?" → "If we take the river route, how would you like to proceed?"
- "What would you need to see before the convoy can pass?"

## What to avoid

- **"I understand…" in response to anger.** Do not say "I understand your situation/position" to an angry or hostile statement. CCHN warns that an emotion can only be felt, not understood, and claiming to understand it can provoke more aggression (Tool 8, FM pp. 184–185).
- **Apologising to calm an outburst.** CCHN advises against apologising for your position under emotional pressure, because it rewards the use of emotion (FM p. 188). An accusation audit can slide into exactly this.
- **Volunteering accusations that politicise the meeting.** "You probably think we are spies" can pull the conversation to the political level (WHO are you, WHY are you here) before you are ready (Tool 4).
- **Using techniques mechanically across cultures.** CCHN notes that the receiver's perception decides how behaviour is read (FM p. 178). Mirroring or humour can read as mockery.
- **Treating questions as a lever to extract concessions.** This turns a relational negotiation into a transactional one (Tool 4).

## How this relates to CCHN

**Where it agrees** (CCHN Tool 8, "Addressing the Human Elements", de-escalation steps, FM pp. 182–188):
- **Effective pause** matches Step 1: pause "up to 7 seconds, depending on culture" (FM p. 184).
- **Labelling** matches Step 1, where you acknowledge the emotion in words ("I hear you", "I hear your suspicions", FM p. 184), and Step 3, where you capture the emotion to set it aside ("I can see that you are suspicious…", FM p. 186).
- **"That's right" summaries** match Step 2: reformulate with a tag question such as "Am I right?" (FM p. 185).
- **Calibrated questions** match Step 5's open/closed/open sequence (FM p. 187). The answers are not yet options to negotiate. They help the counterpart reason away from the emotion.
- **Listening first** matches Tool 6: actively perceiving matters more than actively persuading, so list the counterpart's points (FM p. 162).

**Where CCHN differs**
- **Purpose.** Voss's toolkit serves a negotiator seeking the best terms, often in adversarial or commercial settings. CCHN treats humanitarian negotiation as relational: its de-escalation steps are emergency measures to bring the meeting back to productive ground (FM p. 188), not leverage, and co-ownership matters more than the result (FM p. 162).
- **"Why".** Voss avoids asking "why" out loud. CCHN's WHAT/HOW/WHY (Tool 9) is an analysis framework for you and your team, not a script. You explore motives with open, non-judgmental questions and treat your conclusions as assumptions. CCHN also expects you to *answer* WHY questions ("Why are you here?") when a counterpart asks them at the political level (Tool 4).
- **Transparency.** CCHN builds trust on clarity and predictability (Tool 3). Techniques that work only if the counterpart does not notice them undermine that.
- **Bargaining tactics** from the book's later chapters are outside CCHN and fit access negotiations poorly.

**Use instead:** Tool 8 for hostility; Tool 6 for listening and options; Tool 9 for analysing what you hear.

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "I understand your frustration, but you have to let us in."
* *Try:* "I hear you." [pause] "You have heard nothing from us for three days. Am I right?"

* *Instead of:* "Why won't you let the convoy through?"
* *Try:* "What would you need to see before the convoy can pass?"

* *Instead of:* "I'm so sorry, we will do better next time."
* *Try:* "I hear your anger about the delay. How can we improve the timing of deliveries, in your view?"

## How KROSS uses it

- **`acknowledges_emotion`** counts only acknowledgement in words that does not adopt the emotion ("I hear you", "I hear your suspicions"). "I understand your situation/position" said to anger does not count. It lowers `hostility` and builds `trust`.
- **`asks_good_question`** rewards open, information-seeking questions. Together with rising `trust`, these questions unlock the counterpart's `hidden_interests` in iceberg order: tactical reasoning first, then motives and values.
- **`reframes_core_issue`** rewards restating the issue as a shared problem, often with a tag question.
- **`offers_options`** rewards the open/closed/open sequence and bringing several options.
