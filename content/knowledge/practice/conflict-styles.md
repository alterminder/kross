# Conflict-Handling Styles (Thomas-Kilmann)

## What it is for

Most people have one or more default ways of handling conflict; knowing yours shows when it helps and when it hurts. CCHN's Facilitator Handbook gives this a 90-minute session (Session 12, "Knowing your Conflict-Handling Style"), so that participants get familiar with the five styles and determine their own naturally dominant ones. It uses the Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI), a published self-assessment by Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann. This note does not reproduce the questionnaire.

## The method

**Two axes**
- **Assertiveness:** how much you try to satisfy your own concerns.
- **Cooperativeness:** how much you try to satisfy the other side's concerns.

**Five modes**
- **Competing:** high assertiveness, low cooperativeness.
- **Collaborating:** high on both.
- **Compromising:** moderate on both.
- **Avoiding:** low on both.
- **Accommodating:** low assertiveness, high cooperativeness.

**When each style fits (FH annex to Session 12, paraphrased)**
- *Competing:* quick, decisive action is needed, or an important but unpopular issue is at stake.
- *Collaborating:* both sides' concerns are too important to compromise, you need insights from different perspectives, you want commitment to the solution, or it is a long-term major issue.
- *Compromising:* the goals matter moderately but are not worth the disruption of a more assertive mode; two equally strong parties have mutually exclusive goals; you need expediency; or you need a back-up when competing or collaborating fails.
- *Avoiding:* the issue is trivial, you have no chance of getting what you want, the risk of confrontation outweighs the benefit, or there is a better place to resolve it.
- *Accommodating:* the issue matters much more to the other party, you need to limit the damage of continued conflict, you want to bank goodwill, or you are in the wrong.

**Four questions for choosing a style (FH Session 12)**
1. What, and how much, is at stake?
2. How much power or influence do I have?
3. What is the relationship worth?
4. How interdependent am I with the counterpart?

**The reflection exercise (FH Session 12)**
1. In pairs, each person shares one conflict they handled well and one they handled badly.
2. Each person fills in the self-assessment.
3. The facilitator discusses the five styles.
4. Each person reflects on what they could have done differently, then discusses in pairs which style they could have applied.

The Handbook stresses that you can have several dominant styles and that "There is not a wrong or a correct style."

## At the table

Styles are choices you make move by move, not fixed personality types. In a humanitarian negotiation they often look like this:

- **Collaborating:** exploring the Common Shared Space together. "What would a good arrangement look like from your side?"
- **Compromising:** trading within your bottom line on a Tier 2 issue. "If the route is confirmed by Wednesday, we can deliver on Thursday."
- **Avoiding, as a deliberate deferral:** parking a Tier 3 issue, or declining a diversionary question for now. "That is an important question. I suggest we come back to it once the first convoy is through."
- **Accommodating:** a low-cost goodwill step that matters more to them than to you. "We can hold the meeting at your office instead of ours."
- **Competing, kept assertive and not aggressive:** a clear, reasoned statement of a limit once the dialogue is developed. "This is something I cannot agree to. Let me explain why, and what we can offer instead."

## What to avoid

- **Treating your self-assessment result as a score or a fixed label.** The Handbook frames it as self-knowledge.
- **Using your style as an excuse.** "I'm just a competing type" is not a reason to push.
- **Letting accommodation slide past your bottom line.** Concessions beyond it need instructions from your mandator.
- **Letting competing tip into aggression:** threats, ultimatums, public denunciation.
- **Avoiding a direct question you could answer.** Deliberately deferring a hard issue is different from dodging.
- **Copying or circulating the TKI questionnaire.** It is a third-party published instrument.

## How this relates to CCHN

- **Not a CCHN tool.** The Field Manual does not cite Thomas-Kilmann, and Facilitator Handbook Session 12 cites no Field Manual pages. CCHN uses the model for self-reflection, in line with the Core attitude "Self-awareness about one's perceptions" in the Competence Chart (FM p. 381).
- **CCHN bases its tactical choices on analysis, not personal style.** Your style tells you what you will tend to do. The CCHN tools tell you what the situation calls for:
  - **Typology (Tool 4):** political negotiations seek "the right compromise" on values, professional ones build consensus on method, and technical ones share information and expertise (FM pp. 112–113).
  - **Tiers (Tool 14):** quick wins first, then issues that test the counterpart's bottom line, then hard issues parked for the mandators (FM p. 305). This is close to accommodate, then compromise, then deliberately avoid.
  - **Assertive vs. aggressive (Tool 8):** CCHN accepts assertiveness and warns that aggressiveness imposes a position through emotional leverage (FM pp. 178–180). It also warns that the weaker party's aggression is likely to trigger escalation (FM p. 181).
  - **Not answering a diversionary question (Tool 4):** a legitimate, CCHN-endorsed form of avoiding (FM p. 127).
- **Compromise has limits.** In CCHN, compromises on principles and red lines are the mandator's call, not a matter of style (Tool 17).

## Try instead

* *Instead of (over-accommodating under pressure):* "Fine, let's just do it your way."
* *Try:* "Let me check I have your points right, and then we can look at a few options together."

* *Instead of (competing that turns aggressive):* "We are not leaving until you sign."
* *Try:* "This matters a great deal to us. What would you need to be able to agree on the first step today?"

## How KROSS uses it

- **No style score.** KROSS does not score conflict styles. You can use the model in the debrief to understand your patterns.
- **Moves that show a style:**
  - collaborating: `offers_options`, `proposes_shared_objective`;
  - competing that has become aggressive: `positional_demand`, `threatens`, `aggressive_emotional_leverage`;
  - accommodating too far: `unauthorized_commitment`, which raises `mandate_breach` or `red_line_crossed`;
  - avoiding gone wrong: `evades_direct_question`, as distinct from deliberately deferring an issue (`refers_to_mandator`).
- **Dials.** `hostility` and `rigidity` show how the counterpart responds to the style you choose.
