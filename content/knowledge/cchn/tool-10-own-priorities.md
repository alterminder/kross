# CCHN Tool 10: Identifying Own Priorities and Objectives

## What it is for

Tool 9 reads the counterpart. Tool 10 does the same for your own organization. It turns the mandate and the context into clear priorities and objectives for one specific negotiation. It also builds an argument you can explain to the counterpart at any level. It prepares the transactional stage and the Common Shared Space (pp. 218-219).

## The method

**Start from the mandate (pp. 220-222).** The mandator (the organization's hierarchy) authorizes the mandatee (the negotiator) to negotiate in the organization's name and for its benefit. The mandate sets objectives and limits, expected methods and reporting lines. Unlike ordinary instructions to staff, it gives the negotiator a high degree of autonomy within those limits. That autonomy is space to explore options and find the best possible outcome. Section 3 of the manual (Tools 15-17) covers mandates in detail.

**Ask three questions, bottom-up (p. 222)**

1. **WHY** does our organization want to operate in this context? What are our principles, motives and values?
2. **HOW** will we put these values into practice? Which problems are we addressing, and with which professional tools and methods? What is the reasoning of the operational plan?
3. **WHAT** is our position in this negotiation? What is our offer of service, and on what terms will we operate? This is the point of departure, the best-case scenario of an agreement.

**Build the iceberg in reverse (p. 223; Figure 5, p. 221).** You read the counterpart from the top down, because you can only infer their reasoning and motives from what they say. Your own iceberg is built from the bottom up. Your values are known, and nobody needs to guess them (p. 225). They shape your methods and professional standards, and those shape the opening position you communicate. The manual calls this process inductive, and the counterpart analysis deductive (p. 217).

**Make it explainable at every level (p. 223).** Build the iceberg so you can explain your position from any angle at any point in the negotiation. This lets you move between types of negotiation (Tool 4):
* **Political:** about values and identity. *WHO are you? WHY are you here?*
* **Professional:** about methods. *HOW do you operate?*
* **Technical:** about modalities. *WHAT do you need? WHERE will you work? WHEN will you start?*

**Negotiator Handbook steps (pp. 47-50)**
* Name your values, motives, fears and hopes, and work out how to unpack and translate them so the counterpart finds them palatable.
* Explain in lay terms how you will apply those values in this context.
* State a position that can be communicated in clear and predictable terms.

The worksheet (p. 50) has the same three rows. WHO/WHY leads to values and motives, HOW to tactical reasoning, and WHAT to positions.

The manual's worked example (pp. 224-228) fills these rows for Health for All, whose staff are held after a labour dispute. Values: principles, equitable access to care, medical ethics. Methods: medical protocols, community consultation, accountability to health authorities. Positions: staff release, safety guarantees, a scaled-down handover, consultation to rebuild trust.

## At the table

* Know your opening position, and know why you hold it and how you will deliver it. Then you can answer a challenge at any layer.
* Answer at the level you are asked. If the counterpart asks *"Who are you to come here?"*, a technical reply about trucks misses the point. If they ask *"When do you start?"*, a speech on principles misses it too.
* Use plain words for values: *"We are a medical organization. We are here because people in this district cannot reach surgery, and we treat patients by need alone."*
* Explain methods as practical reasons: *"We assess needs on the spot so that help goes where it is most needed."*
* Keep the position clear and predictable (NH p. 48).

## What to avoid

* Staying in advocacy mode. The tool aims to move the team from defending its position to building co-ownership of the options (p. 229).
* Being unable to explain your position from every angle (p. 223).
* Reciting principle labels without translating them (NH p. 47; see Tool 11).
* Forgetting that the mandate's autonomy has limits. Frontline negotiation is a mandate with considerable autonomy but also with red lines (p. 221).

## Key lines from the manual

> "Frontline humanitarian negotiation is a specific mandate given to designated staff that comes with considerable autonomy—but also with red lines." (p. 221)

> "The logic of building one's own iceberg is the reverse of interpreting the position of the counterpart." (p. 223)

> "build the organization's iceberg in such a way as to be able to explain its position in a negotiation through the various angles" (p. 223)

> "shifting the mindset of the negotiating team from advocating for one's position to finding ways to build co-ownership on the negotiation options" (p. 229)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* (asked "Why are you here?") "We are a neutral, impartial and independent humanitarian organization, and we have a mandate."
* *Try:* "We are here because families in the camps are short of food. We do not take sides in the fighting, and we give help according to need."

* *Instead of:* "Our headquarters requires full access, so that is our position."
* *Try:* "We would like to start distributions in the two camps next week. To do that well, we need to assess needs on the ground. Can we discuss how that could work for your administration?"

## How KROSS uses it

* `trainee_brief.objective` and `trainee_brief.mandate` are your WHAT and your limits. `mandate.can_decide` is where you have autonomy. `mandate.must_refer` is where you must consult your mandator.
* `trainee_brief.red_lines` (with their source: legal, institutional, professional or ethical) mark the outer limits that come with that autonomy.
* `unpacks_principle` rewards explaining values in the counterpart's terms. `argues_principles_only` penalizes reciting them, which raises rigidity and lowers trust.
* `unauthorized_commitment` triggers the `mandate_breach` or `red_line_crossed` flags when you commit beyond your mandate.
* Counterparts may switch levels and ask *who are you and why are you here?* A coherent own iceberg helps you keep trust and consistency.
* Debrief question: *What was your organization's objective?*
