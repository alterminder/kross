# CCHN Tool 6: Creating a Conducive Environment for a Transaction

## What it is for

At the transactional stage the parties agree on the terms of the exchange and decide whether to accept its costs and risks (p. 156). Two people sit at the table, but mandators, hierarchies, donors, conservative forces on both sides, other agencies and political authorities are all "breathing down the necks" of the negotiators (pp. 156-157). Tool 6 gives practical steps and a checklist to make the meeting work despite that pressure.

## The method

**Know what is being bundled** (pp. 158-159). Agencies "bundle" elements to keep control of risk (e.g. camp access plus collecting data on violations). Counterparts may "underline, rephrase, or obscure" terms to lower their own costs. Vagueness "can quickly turn into a liability" later. The skill is to specify what implementation needs and leave aside contentious points that matter little operationally.

**Prepare** (pp. 160-161):
- Understand the counterparts' power structure, their stakeholders, and the personal and institutional liabilities they carry.
- Consult your own key internal stakeholders in advance.
- Select the issues beforehand and build the agenda on areas of convergence. The agenda should identify the issues, propose a path for the discussion and set a process toward implementation.
- Treat the meeting as "an opportunity for dialogue rather than a moment of resolution and arbitrage". The counterpart may well decide later.
- Map the Common Shared Space: flexibility vs. red lines.

**Conduct** (pp. 161-163):
- **Focus on the people** at the desk, in the room and outside it. Identify "those who are diverting attention from the ones who are deciders".
- **Be predictable.** The less improvised the meeting, the more confidence it builds.
- **Listen** and take the counterparts' points into account explicitly. Watch your body language.
- **Let them take the initiative.** Co-ownership matters more than the result.
- **Actively perceive rather than persuade.** List their points and make sure you understand them from their perspective.
- **Bring options, not solutions.** The manual's example is access by road vs. by river vs. by air.
- **Create shared value before claiming benefits.**
- **Be transparent about red lines "when some of these options are unlikely to be agreed on"**, to avoid false expectations. Postpone difficult terms and revisit them later.
- **End with next steps** and implementation contacts.
- **Thank the counterparts**, "even if it does not end in an agreement" (p. 163).

**The predictability box** (p. 161). The manual calls predictability and managing expectations "by far the best factors" for success in contentious negotiations. An unexpected change of position on a central issue can damage the relationship "even if the change favors the interests of the counterpart".

**The checklist to prepare, conduct and debrief a transaction meeting** (pp. 164-165; NH pp. 30-31):
- *Preparing:* stakes for all parties, who attends, what they know about you, convergence and divergence, an agenda prepared and shared, setting, timing, translator, evidence, protocol.
- *Proposed terms:* movable pieces (bottom lines) in time, geography, priority and standards; points of no flexibility (red lines); benefits for the counterparts; an action plan.
- *Power structure:* who is in charge, who they report to, how much flexibility they have.
- *At the meeting:* Who is in the room? Who is talking? Who are the deciders? Who are the diverters?
- *Debrief:* Can we list their points? Did we discuss them on their own terms? Did we present our objectives as shared ones? Did we raise options when we met resistance? Are the next steps clear?

## At the table

- Open on the shared problem: "Everyone here is worried about the food situation in the camp. Can we look at it together?"
- Reflect their points before adding yours: "So your concerns are the timing near the front line and who drives the trucks. Have I missed anything?"
- Offer options: "We could go by road, by river, or by air. Which would work best from your side?"
- Close: "Let us agree on next steps. We send you the convoy details by Thursday. Who should our logistician call?"

## What to avoid

- Claiming before creating value, for example telling the counterpart it is their "moral and legal obligation" to let you in (p. 162).
- Bringing a single solution and defending it.
- Leaving terms vague to get a quick yes (p. 159).
- Changing position on a central issue without warning, even in the counterpart's favour (p. 161).
- Stating red lines as opening absolutes. Tool 6 endorses transparency about red lines once an option is unlikely to be agreed. That is different from door-closers such as "non-negotiable" or "We are not willing to discuss this point", which the manual warns against (p. 240).
- Body language that betrays opposing feelings (p. 161).

## Key lines from the manual

> "The point is not to seek everyone's support at the transactional stage, but to be transparent about the efforts of the organization" (p. 157)

> "The point of frontline negotiation is more about maintaining and developing relationships than seeking specific outcomes." (p. 161)

> "Co-ownership about the results of the meeting is more important than the results themselves." (p. 162)

> "Actively perceiving, which is more important than actively persuading." (p. 162)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "We need immediate access to save lives! It is your moral and legal obligation to allow us in."
* *Try:* "We want to address together the food crisis that is raising concerns on all sides. How do you see it?"
* *Instead of:* "The convoy will come on Tuesday by the main road."
* *Try:* "We see a few ways to reach the camp: road, river or air. What are the pros and cons from your side?"
* *Instead of:* ending with "We'll wait to hear from you."
* *Try:* "Thank you for your time. Can we agree on two next steps and who to contact for each?"

## How KROSS uses it

- **asks_good_question** is "perceive before persuade". **evades_direct_question** is its opposite: ignoring the counterpart's point.
- **proposes_shared_objective** (shared value before claiming) and **offers_options** (options, not solutions) lower **rigidity**. **argues_principles_only** ("moral and legal obligation") raises it.
- **unexpected_position_change** is the predictability box. It costs **trust**.
- **premature_red_line** covers door-closing absolutes. A limit stated later, when an option is clearly unlikely, does not count. That follows Tool 6.
- Preparation maps onto **trainee_brief.mandate**, **trainee_brief.red_lines**, **issues** (bottom_line vs. red_line) and **counterparts[].authority** (who decides). **stakeholders**, where listed, are the people behind the table. **implementation_checklist** holds the terms to pin down before next steps.
