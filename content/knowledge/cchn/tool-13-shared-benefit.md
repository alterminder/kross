# CCHN Tool 13: Identifying the Shared Benefit of the Negotiation

## What it is for
Any agreement is "a bundle of compromises" and can be attacked as a "bad deal" unless it shows a benefit for both sides (p. 278). Tool 13 helps the team name the shared benefit, rank compromises by benefit and risk, find where further compromise stops being worth it (the bottom line), and keep that line separate from the mandate's red lines. The Negotiator Handbook merges this tool with FM Tool 14 (NH p. 64, footnote).

## The method

**Red lines vs bottom lines (box, p. 279)**
- **Red lines** are the outer limits of an agreement. They are set in the mandate, informed by law and institutional policy, and cannot be crossed. A breach can undermine the agreement's validity and the legitimacy of the negotiator and organisation, and may bring legal liability (e.g. counter-terrorism law). The negotiator may not set or revise them.
- **Bottom lines** are the negotiator's own tactical tool for stopping the conversation when options show rising risk and falling benefit. Before going further you may consult your hierarchy or stakeholders, which may move the bottom line.

**Steps (FM pp. 278-287; NH pp. 63-69)**
1. **Ideal outcome (A).** The top of your own iceberg: maximum benefit, little or no compromise, but "unrealistic" because it ignores the counterpart and holds no shared benefit (p. 280). The counterpart has its own A'.
2. **Shared benefit.** As both sides move from their ideals, a "shared benefit window" opens (p. 281). Benefits differ in kind. For you: impact on the population, leadership in the field. For the counterpart: security, economic or political interests, such as control over relief or legitimacy with its own hierarchy (pp. 278-279).
3. **Compromise ladder.** Rank the compromises. In the FM example (pp. 282-283), Food Without Borders (FWB) wants full access to an IDP camp with no military present (A). Then: (1) military present but not visible, a "quick gain"; (2) military actively monitoring distribution, which affects how neutral and independent FWB looks but gives the camp commander visible control; (3) limited access with a military escort and a beneficiary list for the authorities, where benefit starts to fall. Past the point of maximum shared benefit the talk "again becomes a zero-sum game" (p. 282). The NH tiers are 1 "Easy to agree", 2 "More demanding" (highest shared benefit), 3 "Harder to agree" (NH p. 66).
4. **Rising risks.** Sources of risk: efficiency of distribution and other professional standards, humanitarian principles, reputation, staff security, protection of the population, and other legal norms such as counter-terrorism law (p. 284). The NH rates each position low, medium or high (NH p. 67).
5. **Bottom line (B).** Where falling benefit crosses rising risk. Here you pause, reflect with your team and take further compromise to the mandator (p. 285).
6. **Red line (C).** Fixed by policy across contexts, not by this negotiation. It may be "slightly modified" with the mandator but is unlikely to move much, even for a high humanitarian return (p. 286). A red line can sit above the equilibrium point (the ICRC's ban on military escorts); then a bottom line on that issue is pointless.

**The three zones (Figure 17, p. 287; NH p. 69)**
- A to B: **"Open dialogue"**. Explore and agree.
- B to C: **"Requires instructions"**. Pause and refer to the mandator.
- Beyond C: **"Outside mandate"**.

## At the table
- Name the benefit for them: "If distribution runs smoothly, your office can show the camp is calm and families are fed. That helps both of us."
- When an option passes your bottom line, pause without closing the door: "This goes further than I can assess alone. I'd like to take it back to my team and come back to you."
- If the counterpart cites a more flexible organisation, do not debate your policy: "Each organisation works under its own rules, and ours are set above my level. Let's look at what we can do on monitoring."

## What to avoid
- Presenting your ideal outcome as realistic, or not showing the counterpart what it gains (p. 278).
- Drifting into "an increasingly compromising and risky posture while the shared benefits and ownership of the negotiation keep shrinking" (p. 285).
- Confusing your bottom line (yours to set) with a red line (the mandator's).
- Buckling when compared with organisations that accept more risk: the negotiator "does not have permission to engage on" that policy decision (p. 287).

## Key lines from the manual
> "red lines are defined as the outer limits of the possible areas of an agreement." (p. 279)

> "Bottom lines are under the control of the negotiator as a means to suspend or postpone considerations of additional options below a certain threshold of possibilities." (p. 279)

> "The bottom line is situated when and where the diminishing return crosses the rising risk, calling for a pause in the dialogue with the counterpart" (p. 285)

> "Yet, the negotiator with the more restrictive organization should not buckle to the pressure." (pp. 286-287)

## Try instead
* *Instead of:* "Other agencies may accept escorts, but that's their problem. We won't discuss it."
* *Try:* "Our policy on escorts is decided by our headquarters, not by me. What I can work on with you is how your team keeps order around the distribution."
* *Instead of:* "Fine, we'll hand the beneficiary list to your officers." (a tier-3 compromise agreed on the spot)
* *Try:* "Sharing names raises protection questions I need to take back to my team. Can we first agree on how many families are registered and where distribution happens?"

## How KROSS uses it
- **issues**: each issue has `ideal`, `bottom_line` and `red_line` for the trainee and the counterpart (hidden), plus a `tier` (1/2/3): A, B and C on the Tool 13 scale.
- **trainee_brief.mandate**: `can_decide` matches open dialogue (A to B), `must_refer` matches "requires instructions" (B to C). **trainee_brief.red_lines** is "outside mandate"; `flexible_by_mandator_only` reflects that only the mandator moves a red line.
- **Moves**: `proposes_shared_objective` (naming a benefit for both sides); `refers_to_mandator` (the right move at the bottom line); `unauthorized_commitment` (agreeing past B or C, raising `mandate_breach` or `red_line_crossed`).
- **Dials**: showing the counterpart its benefit lowers `rigidity`, builds `trust`.
