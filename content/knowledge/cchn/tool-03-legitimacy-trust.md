# CCHN Tool 3: Fostering Legitimacy and Building Trust

## What it is for
As humanitarian work shifts from entitlement to engagement, the relationship becomes "the central asset to cultivate" (p. 90). The manual takes a two-step approach (p. 90):
1. identify the negotiator's **sources of legitimacy** and fit them to the counterpart;
2. build the counterpart's **trust** in those sources.

Legitimacy depends on how the counterpart sees three things (pp. 90-91):
- the negotiator's character and profile;
- the organization's mission, mandate and track record;
- how relevant the negotiation's objectives are to the situation.

The test is always "in the eyes of the counterpart" (p. 104).

## The method
**Five sources of legitimacy** (Figure 4, pp. 93-95):
1. **Institutional representation**: *Where is the negotiator coming from?* The organization's mission and reputation, as shown by job title, staff, offices and vehicles. Your assignment to negotiate is narrower than the organization's mandate.
2. **Topic or contextual expertise**: *What does the negotiator know about this context or theme?*
3. **Personal legitimacy**: *Who is the negotiator?* Gender, age, marital status, ethnicity, religion, self-confidence, charisma, self-awareness.
4. **Adaptability**: *How can the negotiator adapt to new situations?* Empathy, adjusting one's behavior, staying neutral and present in the conversation.
5. **Network connections**: *Who does the negotiator know?*

**Weigh them for this counterpart** (pp. 96-98). The sources do not carry equal weight in every situation.
- **A highly normative negotiation with a traditional, suspicious community leader.** Stress personal features (age, family status), proven adaptability, and ties to scholars and community leaders. Avoid the legal basis of the mandate in international law, the organization's history, its Nobel Prizes, or your own Ph.D. In the manual's words, "the more normative the mission, the more disruptive the mandate will be perceived".
- **A technical or professional counterpart** (an army commander, a hospital director). Stress the organization's reputation, your topic competence, rigor in how you present yourself, and networks of experts. Interpersonal adaptability counts least here.

Map your assets and liabilities with frank colleagues, especially national staff (p. 98). The same model helps choose who should negotiate, even if that means sending someone else (p. 93).

**Three indicators of trust** (pp. 99-100):
- **Clarity.** Present clear messages. Counterparts trust what they can see and measure. Principles and assessments must be "unpacked" into terms the counterpart can accept (p. 99).
- **Adaptability.** Adapt your position to the counterpart's perspective. This takes empathy (understanding how the other side sees and feels) and creativity (rethinking your position to benefit the other side). It starts from the island of agreements (pp. 99-100).
- **Predictability.** Stay predictable as circumstances shift. Keep the space and protocol of the negotiation even when views differ (p. 100).

**Table 1, the 3x3 grid** (p. 101). The rows are what the counterpart judges; the columns are the three indicators:

| | Clarity | Adaptability | Predictability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Negotiator** | Clear about identity, competence, features, network | Adapts to changing circumstances and cultures | Keeps key features and respects protocols over time |
| **Organization** | Clear on mandate, mission, core competences | Adapts the mission to local circumstances | Same image across contexts and over time |
| **Objectives** | Clear on objectives, needs assessment, standards, target groups and services | Shows how activities answer the counterpart's circumstances and expectations | Track record and intent to keep commitments |

The Handbook turns the grid and the five sources into printable templates (NH pp. 16-18).

## At the table
- **Present yourself in the terms this counterpart values.** Lead with the sources that carry weight with them, and keep the least helpful ones out of the conversation (p. 96). This is about adjusting how you communicate, not inventing an identity (p. 92).
- **Explain principles in practical terms instead of reciting them** (p. 104).
- **Keep the same words, priorities and offer across meetings, and show how your plan answers their concerns.**

Example lines:
- "We treat anyone who is sick or wounded, whichever side they are on. In practice, that means no one is turned away at our door."
- "Here is exactly what we propose: one clinic, two doctors, open five days a week. What would you need to see to be comfortable with it?"

## What to avoid
- **Claiming legitimacy from your principles.** It is not enough to say you are neutral and impartial and therefore legitimate. The counterpart has to recognize those qualities in the specific circumstances (p. 91).
- **Ambiguity, rigidity, or changing your story.** Each undermines one of the three pillars (pp. 99-100). Rigid, inflexible people and organizations are rarely trusted (p. 99).
- **Showing the wrong credentials** to a counterpart who will read them as disruption (p. 97).

## Key lines from the manual
> "It does not suffice to argue that because an organization is neutral and impartial it is therefore legitimate." (p. 91)

> "Counterparts will trust only what they can apprehend, see, and measure. Ambiguous people or organizations cannot be trusted." (p. 99)

> "people or organizations who change their mind, the terminology, or their priorities all the time can rarely be trusted." (p. 100)

> "It is about modifying one's communication style more than shaping a new identity." (p. 92)

## Try instead
* *Instead of:* "We are a neutral and impartial organization, so you have no reason to stop us."
* *Try:* "We give care based only on need, and we do not take sides in the fighting. You can check how we have worked in the next district. What concerns would you want us to address first?"

* *Instead of:* (to a traditional leader) "Our mandate comes from the Geneva Conventions, and our organization has won the Nobel Peace Prize."
* *Try:* "Our team includes people from this region, and we have worked alongside community elders in the next valley. We would value your advice on how to work here."

## How KROSS uses it
- **Trust and consistency.** The `trust` dial is grounded in the three indicators. `consistency` tracks whether the trainee keeps a coherent story.
- **Moves that cost trust.** Reciting principles is `argues_principles_only`. Changing position, terms or priorities abruptly is `unexpected_position_change`, even when the change favors the counterpart. Contradicting an earlier fact is `story_contradiction`.
- **Moves that build trust.** Putting a principle in the counterpart's own terms is `unpacks_principle`. Respect and curiosity (`shows_respect_curiosity`) show adaptability.
- **Weighing legitimacy.** `counterparts[].authority` and `counterparts[].iceberg.motives_values` hint at which sources will count.
