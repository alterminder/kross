# CCHN Tool 11: Exploring the Common Shared Space

## What it is for

The Common Shared Space (CSS) is the area where the parties explore possible arrangements. You draw it from the two icebergs: your own (Tool 10) and the counterpart's (Tool 9). It prepares the transactional stage and later informs where you set bottom lines and red lines (p. 230).

The CSS is distinct from the "humanitarian space" (p. 231). Advocates protect the humanitarian space and press the other side to respect entitlements. Negotiators work in a co-owned space where both parties can safely review values, methods and parameters. The more trusting and predictable the relationship, the more fertile it is. This is a shift in ethos, from guardian of the humanitarian space to negotiator (p. 231).

## The method

**The four elements of the space (p. 232)**
1. Communicate the positions of both parties, P and P'.
2. Explain your tactical reasoning (R) and connect it to the counterpart's (R').
3. Be open to discussing your values (V) in language that relates to the counterpart's values and identity (V').
4. Recognize the distance between the two sets of positions, methods and values as an opportunity for dialogue.

The CSS holds all the options, even those one side dislikes. The aim is to understand each side's preferences and objections (p. 232).

**The search areas (Figure 6, p. 233).** Between the two icebergs lie three search areas: Potential Shared Positions (P-P'), Potential Shared Reasoning and Methods (R-R') and Potential Shared Values (V-V').

**Diagnose the level of a disagreement (pp. 235-236).** In the manual's example, a militia leader demands that an NGO remove its logo from trucks. Is the objection about:
* where and when the logo is shown (technical level),
* how the logo is used to identify convoys (tactical or professional reasoning), or
* what the logo means (values)? Here, he finds it offensive to local culture.

Then search where the relationship has most traction: V-V' (religious scholars who can confirm the logo is non-religious), R-R' (local professionals who vouch for its use) or P-P' (flexible visibility, better notification).

**Translate principles and methods (pp. 236-239).** Humanity, impartiality, neutrality and independence belong to the humanitarian community, not to the parties. You must unpack them into a vocabulary the counterpart can recognize (p. 237):

| Principle | Shared vocabulary (FM p. 237) |
|---|---|
| Humanity | Focusing on preserving the life and dignity of people affected by armed violence |
| Impartiality | Considering the needs of those most affected first |
| Neutrality | Refraining from taking sides in armed hostilities |
| Independence | Acting without interference from other actors and stakeholders |

Methods need the same treatment (p. 239). Protection means drawing authorities' attention to their own obligation to protect people, not securing the population yourself. Confidentiality means discretion, "but not secrecy".

**Application in three steps (pp. 244-248).** For shared values, then shared reasoning and methods, then shared positions, list the convergent elements to explore and the divergent elements to leave aside. The Negotiator Handbook adds a final step: draft your starting position in clear, understandable and open terms (p. 56).

**Keep the tools distinct** from the Island of Agreements and the Typology (pp. 241-243; see Related tools).

## At the table

* Open with what your organization wants, not what it rejects (p. 241): *"We would like to bring food to the two camps before the rains."*
* Name a shared value early: *"We both want the families in the camp to stay healthy."*
* Ask where the objection sits: *"Is the concern where the logo appears, how it is used, or what it means to people here?"*
* Translate principles instead of reciting them: *"We do not take sides in the fighting. Our only concern is the people affected by it."*

## What to avoid

* Defending your own iceberg instead of exploring (p. 231).
* Reciting principle labels as rhetoric (p. 237).
* Language that closes the door (p. 240): "Under international law, we have the right to...", "Our organization will never accept...", "This position is non-negotiable.", "We are not willing to discuss this point.", "This situation is unacceptable."
* Denouncing the counterpart yourself. Your role is to converse, explore and build trust. Strong prohibiting messages belong to the mandator; ask your team for that support (p. 240).
* Opening with red lines. Red lines have their time, but only once dialogue and engagement are sufficiently developed (p. 241).
* Rationales the counterpart cannot accept, and obstacles created by positions of principle (p. 247).

## Key lines from the manual

> "To succeed, a negotiation must be more than a competition between two narratives." (p. 231)

> "Humanity, Impartiality, Neutrality, and Independence are values and norms that belong to the humanitarian community, not the parties to an armed conflict." (p. 237)

> "The mandator (e.g., country director of the organization) should be the one communicating the strong prohibiting messages." (p. 240)

> "To start a conversation by stating the red lines is an act of power subjugating the Common Shared Space to the terms of one side." (p. 241)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "Under international law, we have the right to enter this camp."
* *Try:* "We want to reach the families who need help most, first. How could we organize that together with your administration?"

* *Instead of:* "We will never accept armed escorts. This is non-negotiable."
* *Try:* "We travel without weapons so that everyone can see we are not taking part in the fighting. What would help you feel confident about the convoy's movements?"

* *Instead of:* "What your men are doing is unacceptable, and we condemn it."
* *Try:* "Could we look together at how the checkpoint procedure affects the families crossing it?" (and ask your mandator to deliver any formal protest)

## How KROSS uses it

* `premature_red_line` fires on door-closing absolutes ("never", "non-negotiable", "unacceptable", "we are not willing to discuss this") and on opening with what you reject. It raises rigidity.
* `argues_principles_only` fires on "under international law we have the right" and on principles cited without linking them to the counterpart's interests. `unpacks_principle` rewards translation. `proposes_shared_objective` rewards naming common ground first.
* `island_of_agreements` (convergent and divergent norms) and each counterpart's `iceberg` give the material for the CSS.
* `trainee_brief.red_lines` stay real limits; state them only once dialogue is established.
* Debrief question: *Where was the common shared space?*
