# CCHN: Competences of Humanitarian Negotiators

## What it is for

The Competence Chart helps negotiators and their agencies decide which capabilities to invest in (FM pp. 27–28).
- **Origin.** It was developed at a professional consultation in Caux, Switzerland, in June 2019, with 22 experienced field practitioners from the CCHN community, and then discussed in peer workshops (FM p. 380).
- **Status.** CCHN calls it a "first baseline reflection" that is expected to evolve. The elements are "shared objectives" that members aspire to, not a test (FM p. 380).

## The method

**Three categories (FM p. 380)**
- **Knowledge:** concepts and methods, learned through training workshops, reading and experience.
- **Attitudes:** personal behaviours and perspectives, built mostly through self-reflection and critical thinking based on field experience.
- **Skills:** technical abilities to carry out negotiation tasks.

**Three levels (FM pp. 380–382)**
- **Core:** the basic requirements for anyone working, or hoping to work, in this domain.
- **Advanced:** what professionals should aspire to as they handle more complex and demanding negotiations.
- **Expert:** the most advanced elements, for the most experienced negotiators.
- Advanced and Expert levels include everything from the levels below (FM p. 381).

**The chart, as printed (FM p. 381)**

| Level | Knowledge | Attitudes | Skills |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Understanding of humanitarian principles and basic rules; Capacity to analyze contexts and interests; Capacity to analyze networks; Knowledge of cultural protocols | Self-awareness about one's perceptions; Emotional intelligence; Accepting complexity; Intercultural flexibility; Empathy | Ability to think tactically and critically; Ability to work under pressure; Ability to deal with difficult people; Ability to share experience and perspectives openly |
| **Advanced** | Capacity to understand and manage multiple external stakeholders; Capacity to leverage influence in complex environments; Capacity to balance interests and find the right compromises; Capacity to engage in strategic thinking and decision-making; Capacity to reflect and deliberate on complex issues in a systematic manner | Adaptability to changing environments; Ability to create options out of complex problems | Ability to address negotiations tactically, including the use of social media; Ability to work and live in complex environments |
| **Expert** | Capacity to forecast changing environments and positions; Capacity to develop multifaceted strategies for multiple stakeholders; Capacity to leverage competing sources of influence | Ability to take and manage risks; Ability to lead a negotiation process; Ability to align priorities within one's organization and maintain a clear mandate | Ability to mentor and coach negotiation teams in complex environments |

**Scope (FM pp. 382–383)**
- The competences cut across the humanitarian sector.
- Each agency should brief its negotiators on its own policies before mandating them.
- CCHN focuses on the know-how "above and beyond" the policy and normative framework, and recommends getting policy training through your own organisation.

**What the chart does not give you**
- No behavioural indicators.
- No scoring scale or rubric.
- No assessment method.
- The Facilitator Handbook has no learner assessment either. Its sessions state objectives such as "Participants are able to…".

Any score attached to these competences is someone else's construct, not CCHN's.

## At the table

The chart lists capabilities, not phrases. The links below to the tools that exercise them are an interpretation, not CCHN's own mapping.

- **Capacity to analyze contexts and interests (Core):** asking about the counterpart's position, reasoning and motives (Tool 9). Example: "What would need to be in place for you to be comfortable with this?"
- **Ability to deal with difficult people; emotional intelligence (Core):** pausing, acknowledging in words, reformulating (Tool 8). Example: "I hear you. You have waited a long time for an answer. Am I right?"
- **Self-awareness about one's perceptions (Core):** noticing your own frustration before it shows.
- **Ability to create options out of complex problems (Advanced):** offering options rather than a single solution (Tool 6). Example: "We could go by road in the morning, by river, or start with a smaller convoy. What would work best on your side?"
- **Capacity to balance interests and find the right compromises (Advanced):** knowing your bottom line and red lines (Tools 13–14, 17).
- **Ability to align priorities within one's organization and maintain a clear mandate (Expert):** referring back instead of committing beyond your mandate (Tool 15).

## What to avoid

- Treating the chart as a test score or a certification. CCHN presents it as shared objectives (FM p. 380).
- Assuming you are "Advanced" because you show one Advanced item. The higher levels include the lower ones (FM p. 381).
- Mixing up the categories. For example, "Ability to create options out of complex problems" is listed as an Attitude, and "Capacity to balance interests and find the right compromises" as Knowledge.
- Ignoring agency policy, which the chart assumes you are briefed on (FM p. 382).

## Key lines from the manual

> "It is understood that the selection of competences constitutes a first baseline reflection on the shared features of the members of the CCHN community and their profession." (p. 380)

> "these elements of knowledge, attitudes, and skills are mentioned here as a series of shared objectives towards which members aspire to build their competence" (p. 380)

> "Advanced and Expert levels presume inclusion of the elements of the previous levels." (p. 381)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "I scored 4 on empathy, so I am at Advanced level."
* *Try:* "Which competences did this scenario ask of me, and what did I actually say or do that shows them?"

* *Instead of:* "I need to work on my negotiation skills."
* *Try:* "Next time I want to practise one Core skill, dealing with difficult people: pause, acknowledge in words, then reformulate."

## How KROSS uses it

- **Scoring is KROSS's own.** KROSS scores sessions on its own 1–5 rubric; neither CCHN nor the scenario bank defines that scale.
- **Condensed chart.** `rubric.yaml` holds a condensed copy under `competence_levels` and a draft mapping of difficulty to levels; the engine does not use either today, and the copy moves some items between levels and categories. The table above, transcribed from the Field Manual, is the reference.
- **Moves that touch competences:** `identifies_interest` and `asks_good_question` (analysing interests); `acknowledges_emotion` and `reframes_core_issue` (difficult people); `offers_options` (creating options); `refers_to_mandator` rather than `unauthorized_commitment` (a clear mandate).
