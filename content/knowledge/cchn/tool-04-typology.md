# CCHN Tool 4: Determining the Typology of a Humanitarian Negotiation

## What it is for
Different kinds of negotiation need different tactics. This tool helps you see which kind you are in, adapt, and move the conversation on purpose to the level where you are strongest.

## The method
**1. General category** (pp. 106-110):
- **Adversarial**: an imposed power relationship under duress, such as a kidnapping or extortion. The victim's aim is to end it.
- **Transactional**: an exchange of value in a "market", such as buying a car. Also called interest-based. Little relationship is expected afterwards.
- **Relational**: a lasting relationship built through a series of agreements. Humanitarian negotiation with conflict actors is essentially relational (p. 108). The agreements matter mainly as products of the relationship (p. 110).

A relational negotiation can change category (p. 110). If a commander wants money or goods for himself, it turns transactional, at the expense of the relationship. If he uses coercion, it turns adversarial.

**2. The three types of relational negotiation** (pp. 112-113). All three aim at a Common Shared Space (CSS), the range of possible agreements:

| Level | Counterpart's question | Issues | Approach | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A Political | WHO are you? WHY are you here? | Identity, values, principles, norms | Find the right compromise on specific shared values | High |
| B Professional | HOW will you work? | Methods and standards | Build consensus on method among local professionals | Average |
| C Technical | WHAT will you do? WHERE, WHEN, WITH WHOM? | Operational arrangements based on facts and data | Share information and expertise | Low |

- **Risk** means risk to the organization's reputation, perception, security, or exposure to instrumentalization. It rises as the negotiation becomes more political (p. 112).
- **Humanitarian principles** are political-level values (p. 111).
- **A negotiation can start at any level** and move between levels (p. 112).
- **The Handbook's tactics** (NH p. 20): political, cut a deal on visibility and values; professional, build consensus on your standard among local professionals; technical, assert expertise while building the counterpart's ownership.

**3. Politicizing and depoliticizing** (pp. 124-125). Politicizing moves the talk from technical to professional to political. Depoliticizing moves it the other way. Each party tries to negotiate where it has the upper hand. Humanitarians tend to push toward technical issues and governments toward political ones, but "the opposite can be true" (p. 124). Authorities can also bog you down in technicalities such as travel permits.

**4. Start from the counterpart's level.** The counterpart's level normally prevails, because you are the one seeking acceptance and access (p. 128).

## At the table
- **Political questions need value-based answers** (pp. 129-130). If asked who you are and why you are here, explain your mission, principles, what triggered your offer and what you add. Do not answer with how, what or where. Use a prepared narrative in the counterpart's own words (pp. 115-116). Citing international norms opens a political dialogue (p. 131).
- **Professional.** Do not split the difference: one drop of vaccine against two is not settled at 1.5. Seek consensus among professionals, or agree on a process to reach a common standard. Bring a professional colleague (pp. 120, 132).
- **Technical.** Stick to facts and expertise, and agree fixed terms. Avoid drifting into political or professional issues (pp. 123, 133).
- **When the counterpart politicizes mid-way**, the manual's vaccination case gives three options (p. 134):
  1. stay technical;
  2. move up to professional (suspend and seek guidance from the national authority);
  3. move up to political, which may validate the leader as a political spoiler.

  In that case the manual advises staying technical (p. 135).
- **When caught off guard**, you need not answer the diversionary question. You can suspend, ask for time to consult colleagues, and offer to continue at the previous level (p. 127).

Example lines:
- "Can we focus on how we can work together and provide the necessary assistance to the population in need?" (p. 124)
- "There is a measles outbreak here. We have the vaccines and the teams. All children need the vaccine to stop it." (after p. 135)

## What to avoid
- **Assuming political means high level.** A political negotiation can happen at a checkpoint (pp. 116-117). Seniority can even be a liability.
- **Cutting deals on identity, norms or values** (removing the logo, not mentioning international humanitarian law) without consulting management within clear red lines (p. 116).
- **Value-based arguments you cannot back.** There is "no point" claiming an entitlement in a political negotiation if you cannot compromise on the organization's profile and footprint (p. 125).
- **Handling an issue at the wrong level.** Do not treat a corridor's timing as a matter for consensus or for shared principles. Do not let a field logistician remove items from surgical kits (pp. 126-127).

## Key lines from the manual
> "The level of the counterpart normally prevails since the humanitarian organization is seeking acceptance and access from this counterpart." (p. 128)

> "One should be aware that “political negotiation” does not necessarily mean “high level” negotiation." (p. 116)

> "Engaging the conversation at the level where one party can exert the most influence is often the main objective of the discussion." (p. 125)

> "humanitarian negotiators are not bound to answer the new, diversionary question." (p. 127)

## Try instead
* *Instead of:* (asked "Why are you here?") "We need three trucks through the north road by Tuesday."
* *Try:* "We are here because children in this district are at risk from the outbreak. Our work is to vaccinate every child who needs it, whichever community they come from."

* *Instead of:* (asked to remove logos) "Fine, we will take the logos off everything."
* *Try:* "I take that concern seriously. Let me discuss options with my management and come back to you with a proposal."

## How KROSS uses it
- **Scenario fields.** `negotiation.nature` (relational, transactional or adversarial) and `negotiation.start_level` (political, professional or technical) come from this tool.
- **Mandate.** When a scenario lists deals on identity or visibility under `trainee_brief.mandate.must_refer`, pausing to consult is `refers_to_mandator`. Agreeing alone is `unauthorized_commitment` and raises `mandate_breach`.
- **Answering at the right level.** A logistics reply to WHO/WHY may count as `evades_direct_question`. Pinning down technical terms is `clarifies_operational_detail`. Claiming entitlements is `argues_principles_only`, which raises `rigidity`.
