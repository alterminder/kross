# CCHN Tool 8: Addressing the Human Elements of the Transaction

## What it is for

The transactional stage puts the parties under "considerable pressure", and emotion "can easily derail a meeting" (p. 178). The negotiator has to read the human, cultural and social situation, control their own tone, and know how to bring a meeting back once the counterpart's emotion takes over.

## The method

**Assertive vs. aggressive** (p. 178).
- *Assertive:* communicating a position "using strongly worded reasoning while being respectful of the other side's views".
- *Aggressive:* imposing a position by leveraging an emotion (pride, anger, sadness, pity) or through disrespectful acts or words.
- The receiver's perception decides which one it is. Across cultures, even humor or familiarity can come across as aggressive.

Assertiveness projects the mission, the expected results, the seriousness of the situation and the organization's commitment. It builds "on the authority and responsibility of the counterparts" (pp. 179-180). Aggressiveness works through **emotional leverage** (anger, sarcasm, humor, fear, threats, guilt). It hijacks empathy, can look like a lack of control or authority, invites escalation and damages long-term trust (p. 180). The manual's aggressive example calls the situation "utterly unacceptable" and invokes HQ and the Foreign Office (p. 181).

**Power matters** (p. 181). Aggressiveness from the weaker party will most likely trigger escalation from the dominant side. Aggressiveness from the dominant side expresses power and frustration and undermines trust. In that case, "the only option is to seek a de-escalation".

**The six-step de-escalation protocol** (pp. 182-188; NH pp. 38-39). The cycle is credited in the manual as "inspired by the work of ADN Group" (Paris) (p. 183).
1. **Pause and acknowledge the emotion without getting involved.** Pause "up to 7 seconds, depending on culture". Then respond *in words*: "I hear you." / "I hear your suspicions." (p. 184)
2. **Reformulate** to pull the issue out of the emotion, with a tag question: "We can be easily misinformed if we do not have access to all the information. Am I right?" This replaces escalation with "connivance", a small space of agreement (pp. 185-186).
3. **Capture the emotion and set it aside:** "I can see that you are suspicious of what we bring you… We are not here to cause trouble, but to work out solutions." (p. 186)
4. **Reframe** without the emotion: "How can we work together in ensuring that the information we bring you is of quality and relevance?" (p. 186)
5. **Open / closed / open questions.** Open: "How would you suggest that we address this risk of misinformation?" Closed: "Are there any other possibilities?" (if yes, ask an open question again). Open: "In case of option 'C,' how would you like to proceed?" The answers are "not yet options to be negotiated". They help rationalize the issue from the counterpart's view (p. 187).
6. **Discuss options.** Reset the dialogue around the most workable of the counterpart's proposals (p. 188).

The steps are "emergency measures" for when emotion spins out of control. Use them "without being formulaic", as a full sequence or as single steps (p. 188).

The Negotiator Handbook adds preparation (NH pp. 36-37). Assess the counterpart's frustration before the meeting. Get information on the contentious issues. Refrain from responding to aggressive points as much as possible. If you must correct a fact, stay calm.

## At the table

The Facilitator Handbook illustration (Annex 13.3, FH p. 137) shows an angry camp representative facing a late food delivery:
- Pause and paraphrase: "I hear you: there is an issue of trust… You need to know when the next delivery will take place. Am I right?"
- Set aside: "I see that it is a very difficult and frustrating situation for your community."
- Reframe: "We need to work together in finding a solution."
- Questions: "How can we improve the timing of the delivery in your view?" → "Will this be sufficient?" → "What can we do together as next steps?"

## What to avoid

- **"I understand your position, your situation"** in response to a negative emotion. It may provoke more aggression, because the counterpart wants you to *feel* the emotion (p. 185). If the emotion is positive and on your side, such as outrage at ill-treatment, saying you understand is fine.
- **Apologizing under pressure.** Depending on the culture, refrain from apologizing for your position during de-escalation, because it rewards emotion (p. 188).
- Answering non-verbally by looking upset, annoyed, fearful or dismissive (p. 184).
- The FH list "What not to say and do in active listening" (p. 137): counter-arguing ("I disagree. Here is why…", "No. This is not true."), pretending to understand ("I understand your situation.", "Thank you for your explanation."), and apologizing ("I am sorry.", "We will do better next time.").
- Matching aggression, threatening via superiors, or using sarcasm.

## Key lines from the manual

> "By pausing the conversation (up to 7 seconds, depending on culture), the weaker/aggressed party has a chance to easily disarm an escalation process" (p. 184)

> "An emotion is not something one can 'understand,' it can only be 'felt.'" (p. 185)

> "one may refrain from apologizing for his/her position or positions taken earlier in the de-escalation process as it rewards the use of emotion in the negotiation." (p. 188)

> "Be cognizant that a negotiation can only take place if both parties reason with each other." (NH p. 37)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "I understand your situation, Commander."
* *Try:* (pause) "I hear you. I hear your suspicions."
* *Instead of:* "No, that is not true. Let me explain why…"
* *Try:* "We can be easily misinformed if we do not have access to all the information. Am I right?"
* *Instead of:* "I'm sorry, we will do better next time."
* *Try:* "I see this is a very difficult and frustrating situation for your community. We need to work together in finding a solution."

## How KROSS uses it

- **acknowledges_emotion** (step 1, with "I hear you") lowers **hostility**. "I understand your situation" said to anger does not count.
- **reframes_core_issue** (steps 2, 4 and 6, including tag questions and not apologizing) also lowers **hostility**. **offers_options** covers step 5.
- **shows_respect_curiosity** is assertiveness built on the counterpart's authority and responsibility.
- **aggressive_emotional_leverage**, **threatens** and **insults_counterpart** are the aggressive side. They cost **trust** and raise **hostility** and **rigidity**. A more powerful counterpart escalates in return, as p. 181 predicts. An insult ends the session.
- **counterparts[].authority** helps judge the power relationship between the parties.
