# CCHN Tool 14: Evaluating Cost-Benefit of Options

## What it is for
Scenario design frames a discussion between the negotiator and the support team on tactics (p. 288). Bottom lines help build a trustful relationship and maximise shared benefit, so both sides can agree "without having to refer back to an external authority" (p. 288). Tool 14 maps both sides' limits onto one picture and turns it into a script for the transaction. The Negotiator Handbook merges it into its Tool 13 (NH p. 64, footnote).

## The method

**Citing your bottom line (p. 288)**
Citing the bottom line lets you pause without breaking the relationship, step out to weigh risk and benefit with your team and, if needed, the mandator, and then, with new instructions, explore options up to the red lines (between B and C). It signals that your interest is falling and you are near the limit of your mandate.

**Your scale and theirs (pp. 289-293)**
In the FM case, camp authorities want Food Without Borders (FWB) to pay guards from a local militia in food rations.
- FWB: **A**, rations go only to IDPs by need and FWB pays laborers of its choice in cash. **B**, general distribution to IDPs is acceptable, and food-insecure family members of guards may be included, but nothing goes directly to guards. **C**, from the mandate: no rations as payment, no direct assistance to armed personnel.
- Anything between B and C "will require further instruction from the mandator" (p. 290); refer it, while explaining FWB's limits to the counterpart (p. 291).
- Counterpart: **A'**, the guards run and are paid from the distribution. **B'**, FWB distributes but must hire the guards and pay them in rations. **C'**, the camp authorities lose all control and can share no benefit with the guards (p. 292).

**Areas D, E and F (Figure 20, p. 293; pp. 294-295)**
The common shared space lies between the two sets of red lines:
- **Area E**: favours both sides within both bottom lines, "a shared space of open dialogue" (p. 294).
- **Area D**: favours mostly you, but the counterpart must refer back to its mandator. More principled, but it costs relationship and time and risks politicisation.
- **Area F**: favours mostly the counterpart, so you must refer back to your hierarchy.
- **Other scenarios**: those outside both sets of red lines "are off the table". Do not spend long on them (p. 295).

**Role of stakeholders (p. 295).** Scenarios often follow from other actors' goals; spoilers' red lines can constrain the counterpart, and donors and other agencies influence you.

**Four application steps (HfA case, pp. 296-306)**
1. Lay down the best possible outcomes on both sides (A and A').
2. Identify both sides' red lines "as a precondition for an arrangement to be agreed to" (p. 299). Consult the mandator on your own red lines first, then deduce the counterpart's. Theirs "will arise as the counterpart reacts to the proposal" (p. 300).
3. Identify shared benefits and bottom lines in the space for dialogue. Build on what lies within the bottom lines and avoid the rest if possible. Issues beyond them "are not off the table but will require new instructions" (p. 302).
4. Discuss a preliminary script with the team, using the transaction model (clarify terms, conducive environment, human elements). Order messages by tier (pp. 305-306):
   - **Tier 1, "Maximizing Shared Benefit (find quick wins on both sides)"**: low cost, high benefit; sets a positive tone.
   - **Tier 2, "More demanding on both sides (test limits of the bottom line of the counterpart)"**: some cost and some benefit; the basis for a fair split.
   - **Tier 3, "Complicated issues to be referred to the mandators (postpone difficult issues)"**: high cost, core of the conflict, near or below red lines; set aside at first.

**Multiparty (pp. 306-308).** In a siege, terms agreed with one party must also fall within the space agreed with the other (Figure 21).

## At the table
- Open with tier 1: "Can we agree first that emergency services restart at the hospital?"
- Test their bottom line with options: "Could the guards take part in distribution as observers, with their families registering like everyone else?"
- Pause at your bottom line: "That takes us further than I can go today. Let me consult my team, and we can meet again tomorrow."
- Park tier 3: "Compensation is important. Let's come back to it once the hospital is running again."

## What to avoid
- Opening with tier 3 issues. This invites "confrontation on the positions that might hijack the negotiation process" (pp. 305-306).
- Committing in area F, or between your own B and C, without referring back (pp. 290-291, 295).
- Pushing hard into area D at the cost of the relationship (p. 294).
- Spending time on options outside both sets of red lines (p. 295).

## Key lines from the manual
> "Referencing the bottom line, the humanitarian negotiator can, without breaking the relationship, inform the counterpart that they will need to pause the conversation" (p. 288)

> "It is up to the humanitarian negotiator to determine if, when, and how to communicate the bottom line to the counterparts." (p. 288)

> "These issues are not off the table but will require new instructions." (p. 302)

## Try instead
* *Instead of:* "Yes, we can give rations to the guards directly." (area F, agreed alone)
* *Try:* "That is beyond what I can agree to today. I will put it to my hierarchy. Meanwhile, can we work on how the guards' families in need are registered?"
* *Instead of:* opening the meeting with compensation and the hospital's future (tier 3)
* *Try:* "Let's start with what we both need now: staff safe and emergency care running."

## How KROSS uses it
- **issues**: `ideal`, `bottom_line`, `red_line` for both sides (the counterpart's hidden) give A/B/C and A'/B'/C'. `tier` 1/2/3 follows the tier system.
- **trainee_brief.mandate**: `can_decide` covers options above your B (areas D and E); `must_refer` covers B to C (area F). Agreeing to a `must_refer` item alone is `unauthorized_commitment` (`mandate_breach`); beyond C it is `red_line_crossed`.
- **Moves**: `refers_to_mandator` (pausing without breaking the relationship), `offers_options` (testing their bottom line), `proposes_shared_objective` (tier 1 openings).
- The counterpart's side of each issue is `hidden`: as in CCHN, its limits surface as it reacts to proposals. **stakeholders** lists the other actors whose limits shape the counterpart's.
- **Dials**: CCHN expects tier 1 openings to ease `rigidity`; opening on tier 3 risks confrontation (`rigidity`, `hostility`).
