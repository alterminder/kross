# CCHN: Humanitarian Principles in Negotiation

## What it is for

The Field Manual treats Humanity, Impartiality, Neutrality and Independence in three ways: as the source of a built-in paradox, as values to translate for the counterpart, and as institutional red lines owned by the mandator. Use them at the table without turning them into slogans.

## The method

**1. The paradox (FM p. 21).** Action rests on non-negotiable principles, yet operations depend on arrangements with counterparts, so access always involves some compromise (FM p. 154). How much is acceptable is set by the red lines, not at the table.

**2. Principles do not make you legitimate on their own (Tool 3, FM pp. 91–92).** Your neutral, impartial and independent character counts only if the parties recognise it. Build legitimacy through its five sources and through clarity, adaptability and predictability, and unpack the principles so the counterpart understands them in context (FM p. 104; NH p. 15).

**3. Principles sit at the political level (Tool 4).**
- Principles are matters of normative identity and values, the political type of negotiation, where risk is highest (FM pp. 111–113). Invoking them opens a political dialogue that can cost you concessions (FM p. 131).
- Deals on identity or norms can seriously harm the organisation, so consult management within clear red lines (FM p. 116).
- Claiming a humanitarian entitlement is pointless if you cannot compromise on your profile and footprint (FM p. 125).

**4. Translate them for the counterpart (Tool 11, FM p. 237; NH p. 53).** Move beyond the rhetoric and explain what each principle means here. CCHN's wording:

| Principle | Shared vocabulary (FM p. 237) |
|---|---|
| Humanity | "Focusing on preserving the life and dignity of people affected by armed violence" |
| Impartiality | "Considering the needs of those most affected first" |
| Neutrality | "Refraining from taking sides in armed hostilities" |
| Independence | "Acting without interference from other actors and stakeholders" |

Methods get the same treatment: confidentiality, for example, is discretion but not secrecy (FM p. 239).

**5. Interpretations vary, and the principles create dilemmas (Tool 17, FM pp. 350–357).** The principles are a major source of institutional red lines, but each organisation interprets them differently (FM p. 351).
- **Humanity.** A narrow reading covers lifesaving only; a broad one adds life-enhancing and rights-promoting work (FM p. 351).
- **Neutrality.** Do not be seen taking sides on the core issues of the conflict, but do take the side of vulnerable groups a party targets, even if you then look less neutral (FM pp. 351–352).
- **Impartiality.** Serve only those you can reach, or hold back until you reach everyone? Which distinctions are acceptable? The mandator sets these terms and stays engaged (FM pp. 353–354).
- **Independence.** The counterpart judges it, so stay sceptical of your own claim; your nationality matters and a diverse team helps (FM pp. 354–357).
- **Who decides.** Institutional norms are under the organisation's control, and the mandator may delegate some flexibility (FM p. 351). Only the mandator changes red lines (FM pp. 363–364).

## At the table

- **Answer WHO and WHY with a narrative in the counterpart's words** before you get to HOW and WHAT (FM pp. 115–116, 130).
- **Translate a principle, then link it to what the counterpart cares about.**
- **If your neutrality is challenged, do not insist.** Explain how you work and how they can check it.
- **If a demand touches a principle, do not renegotiate it yourself.** Name the limit calmly and refer it.

Example lines:
- "Our concern is the life and dignity of the people affected by the fighting, wherever they are."
- "We start with those most affected. How do you see the needs in the villages by the river?"

## What to avoid

- Reciting principle labels as rhetoric (FM p. 237).
- Claiming legitimacy because you are neutral and impartial (FM p. 91).
- Door-closing legal or absolute language, such as "Under international law, we have the right to…" or "This position is non-negotiable." (FM p. 240).
- Legal arguments the counterpart does not recognise (FM p. 349).
- Opening with what you reject, or stating red lines before the dialogue is developed (FM p. 241).
- Assuming your organisation's reading is the only one, or relaxing a red line yourself (FM pp. 351, 363–364).

## Key lines from the manual

> "It does not suffice to argue that because an organization is neutral and impartial it is therefore legitimate." (p. 91)

> "Humanity, Impartiality, Neutrality, and Independence are values and norms that belong to the humanitarian community, not the parties to an armed conflict." (p. 237)

> "In practice, access always entails a form of compromise on humanitarian principles so as to maximize the impact of the activities of the organization." (p. 154)

> "Humanitarian negotiators should, however, remain skeptical about their own claim of independence, especially in the eyes of their counterparts." (p. 354)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "We are a neutral and impartial organization, so you must let us through."
* *Try:* "We do not take sides in the fighting, and we go first to those most affected. What would you need to know about how we work before the convoy moves?"

* *Instead of:* "Under international law, we have the right to reach these people."
* *Try:* "Our aim is to preserve the life and dignity of the people affected in this area. How do you see their situation?"

* *Instead of:* "Fine, we will only serve the names on your list."
* *Try:* "Who receives aid is something I need to discuss with my hierarchy. Could we look together at how to make sure the most affected are reached first?"

## How KROSS uses it

- **Moves.** `unpacks_principle` rewards translating a principle. `argues_principles_only` and `overclaims_norm` penalise citing principles, rights or law without linking them to the counterpart. `premature_red_line` catches early door-closing absolutes. `refers_to_mandator` is right when a demand touches a principle.
- **Red lines.** Principle-based limits sit in `trainee_brief.red_lines` (`source: institutional`, usually `flexible_by_mandator_only`); crossing one raises `red_line_crossed`.
- **Context.** `negotiation.start_level: political` puts the principles most at stake; `island_of_agreements` lists convergent and divergent norms.
- **Dials.** Reciting principles tends to raise `rigidity`; translating them builds `trust`.
