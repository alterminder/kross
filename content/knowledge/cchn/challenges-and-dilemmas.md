# CCHN: Challenges and Dilemmas of Humanitarian Negotiation

## What it is for

Session 1 opens the CCHN peer workshop, whose goal is to explore the challenges and dilemmas of negotiation practice from the practitioners' perspective, without promoting specific approaches (FH p. 4). The Field Manual names the core dilemma of the field: action rests on non-negotiable principles, yet operations depend on arrangements with counterparts (FM p. 21). Naming what made a negotiation hard, and why, is the first step to learning from it.

## The method

**Two definitions (FH p. 8)**

- **Challenge:** a fact that stops you negotiating as well as you expect. Face it, be aware of it, and build a long-term approach with enablers and mitigation measures.
- **Dilemma:** a forced choice between two unsatisfying alternatives, weighing their costs and benefits. You must decide, or operations stop.
- *Example:* not speaking the counterpart's language is a challenge. Choosing between an interpreter from outside the country and a national staff member who would be exposed to political or security pressure is a dilemma.

**What is not a challenge (FH p. 9).** The object of the negotiation (for example, lack of access) and the counterpart's distrust are not challenges in themselves. They are what the negotiation is for: seeking access and building trust. CCHN compares this to a doctor: sick patients are the reason for the profession, not an obstacle to it.

**The exercise (FH p. 9).** Each participant reflects alone on one challenge and one dilemma, explains to the table how they handled them, and each table reports three challenges or dilemmas and three ways to overcome them.

**CCHN Survey 2017 (FH Annex 1, pp. 11–12).** Completed by 158 frontline staff and senior managers; the enablers come from 44 interviews.
- **Recurrent challenges:** difficult interlocutors; security constraints; perceived politicization; difficulty identifying and accessing the right counterpart; lack of knowledge of influencing techniques; insufficient time to prepare.
- **Recurrent dilemmas:** security rules vs. proximity with beneficiaries; denunciation or advocacy vs. silence; impartial vs. conditional assistance; where to compromise on sensitive IHL and human rights issues; whether to engage with "controversial" stakeholders.
- **Enablers:** consult with colleagues; long-term presence; use proxies to influence; reputation of the organisation; define a new strategy; personal qualities and skills; networking in the country; more time to prepare; influence over third parties.
- **What practitioners want:** peer support, mentoring and coaching, checklists and guidelines, among others.
- **Headline results (FH p. 9):** the most typical challenges are difficult interlocutors and security constraints; the recurrent dilemma is security rules vs. proximity; the most common way to overcome a challenge is consulting colleagues; the most efficient enabler is long-term presence.

**Where to look in the Field Manual** (a pointer, not a CCHN table)
- *Difficult interlocutors:* Tool 8 (de-escalation), as the Field Manual's Quick Guide also suggests (FM p. 35).
- *Perceived politicization:* Tool 3 (legitimacy) and Tool 4 (typology).
- *Finding the right counterpart:* Tool 9 (authority test) and Tool 12 (network mapping).
- *Denunciation vs. silence:* the mandator, not the frontline negotiator, delivers strong prohibiting messages (FM p. 240) and makes the call on denunciation (FM p. 363).
- *Impartial vs. conditional assistance:* the mandator sets the terms of impartiality (FM p. 354).
- *Where to compromise:* bottom lines and red lines (Tools 13, 14 and 17).

## At the table

During a negotiation, a dilemma is a signal to pause, not to decide alone.
- A forced choice between two bad options that touches a red line: refer it.
- A challenge, such as the wrong interlocutor or missing information: work on the fact.

Example lines:
- "This is not a choice I can make on my own. I will consult my team and come back to you."

After the session, use four reflection prompts:
1. What fact stopped you negotiating as well as you expected? Was it really a challenge, or the object of the negotiation?
2. What were the two unsatisfying options? What did each cost, and who should have decided?
3. Which enabler would have helped: a colleague, more preparation time, a proxy, a longer presence?

## What to avoid

- Calling the counterpart's refusal or distrust "the challenge". It is the job (FH p. 9).
- Treating a dilemma as if it had one right answer. CCHN offers tools, not prescriptions (FH p. 4).
- Settling a dilemma that touches red lines alone at the table. Red lines change only with the mandator (FM pp. 363–364).
- Retelling real cases with identifiable details. CCHN workshops apply Chatham House rules and decontextualise stories (FH p. 5).
- Reading the 2017 survey as a ranking of what works for you.

## Key lines from the manual

> "A fact that prevents you from negotiating as well as you would expect." (Facilitator Handbook, p. 8)

> "Two unsatisfying alternatives that require you to make a choice by weighing the costs/benefits of the two options." (Facilitator Handbook, p. 8)

> "humanitarian actors find themselves caught between the need to respect humanitarian norms and principles and their role to find the right balance of interests with their counterparts" (Field Manual, p. 21)

> "sick patients are not per se a challenge for the physician but the raison d'être of the profession." (Facilitator Handbook, p. 9)

## Try instead

* *Instead of (in the debrief):* "My challenge was that the commander did not trust us."
* *Try:* "Building his trust was the objective. My challenge was that I did not know who he reports to."

* *Instead of (deciding a dilemma on the spot):* "All right, we will accept the armed escort."
* *Try:* "An escort is not something I can settle here. I will take it to my hierarchy. Meanwhile, can we agree on the route and the timing?"

## How KROSS uses it

- **The coach** can use these definitions to separate challenges from dilemmas and point to the matching tool.
- **Where dilemmas sit in a scenario.** They are often built into `trainee_brief.red_lines` and `trainee_brief.mandate.must_refer`. A hostile counterpart (high `hostility`) plays the "difficult interlocutor".
- **Flags.** `red_line_crossed` and `mandate_breach` mark moments where a dilemma that belonged to the mandator was settled alone.
- **Not a scoring scale.** The survey and the definitions are reflection aids. KROSS's scores are its own construct.
