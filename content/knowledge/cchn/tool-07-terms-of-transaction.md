# CCHN Tool 7: Clarifying the Terms of the Transaction

## What it is for

Tool 7 moves attention from the relationship to the product of the negotiation and its implementation (p. 168), where "the devil often lies in the details". Both negotiators may prefer to leave terms vague so that they can report success, which passes the risk of misunderstanding to the people who must implement the deal (pp. 168-169). A written text also gives better support than an oral arrangement if views diverge later (p. 168).

The manual gives three warnings (pp. 169-171):
- **Feasibility in the field.** A federal minister signs full prison access, but the provinces control the prisons and the visits never start.
- **Include the implementors.** An agreement to fly in vaccines is of little use if the airstrip will close in the rainy season and nobody asked the logisticians.
- **Social impact.** A child demobilization commitment fails because the children, especially girls, felt safer with the group than in camps with no school.

## The method

**The five criteria.** The terms of an agreement are properly set when (p. 172):
1. They are **clearly expressed**, in language both sides understand and relate to.
2. They **define plainly the roles and tasks** of each party.
3. They **recognize the reciprocal and interdependent character** of the commitments, especially their **sequence**: the order of tasks and which tasks depend on others.
4. They **set up a process to handle divergence** during implementation, to preserve the spirit of the agreement as circumstances change.
5. They **recognize the power relationship** between the parties, so that each side's responsibility is calibrated.

These are "by no means objective standards". They are a way to test proposed terms and make the final agreement more resilient (p. 173).

**The Negotiator Handbook steps** (NH pp. 32-33):
1. Identify the risks of misunderstanding or misinterpretation in implementation.
2. Work out mitigation measures against each criterion.
3. Set up a "negotiator's hotline" for misunderstandings during implementation.
4. Prepare for the moment the counterpart fails to deliver. Keep the relationship and do not take sides in implementation disputes.
5. Be ready to defend the agreement against detractors on both sides.

**Worked example in brief: a convoy to a militia-controlled IDP camp** (pp. 173-176).
- *Clear commitments.* The NGO specifies the trucks, cargo, schedule, routes, drivers' names, distribution modalities and target population. In exchange, the militia and military mark route and timing on a map, guarantee safe passage, set checkpoint modalities, refrain from interference, and name field contacts with phone numbers.
- *Roles and sequence.* The plan alternates between the two sides: notification on Day 1, then authorization within four days, then confirmation the day before, then the commander's go-ahead within three hours, the lead driver's call on the morning, checkpoints informed, crossing and delivery with the commander present, and exit by the agreed route.
- *Divergence.* There is a direct link to people with authority and a practical way to resolve problems that puts staff safety first. If disagreement continues, the operation is suspended "without further escalation or reprisals" and a meeting is called.
- *Power and responsibility.* The armed actors guarantee security, brief every member along the route and take responsibility for incidents. The NGO guarantees neutral, impartial delivery and that staff do nothing unrelated to the aid.

**After signature** (p. 177). Rather than forcing every complicated point into the text, set up a process for handling misunderstandings. Power struggles will return. Keep mitigation measures ready and stay open to renegotiation.

## At the table

- Read the deal back: "So we send the truck list and route on day one, and your office confirms within four days. Is that right?"
- Name the people: "Who on your side confirms the day before, and how do we reach them?"
- Agree on what happens when things go wrong: "If there is a problem at a checkpoint, can we agree to stop, call each other and meet, rather than act on our own?"
- Bring in implementors: "Before I confirm the date, our logistician needs to check that the road is passable."

## What to avoid

- Accepting a warm but vague "yes, you will have access" as an agreement.
- Agreements the person in front of you cannot deliver, like the federal minister's (p. 169).
- Committing to logistics your own team has not checked (p. 170).
- Forgetting the counterpart's side of the exchange. Terms must be reciprocal.
- Assuming the agreement removes the power imbalance. It "tends to put both sides on an equal footing", but the counterpart will try to keep its dominance (p. 177).

## Key lines from the manual

> "At times, negotiators may share a common interest in leaving ambiguities in the terms of the agreement" (pp. 168-169)

> "a minimum of predictability is essential to the implementation of the agreement, especially in tense and evolving circumstances." (p. 176)

> "the power relationship between the parties will remain a key driver of the implementation phase." (p. 177)

> "Maintain by all means the relationship with the counterpart and refrain from taking sides around disagreements in the implementation phase." (NH p. 33)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "Good, so we have access. Thank you."
* *Try:* "Thank you. Let us write down what we agreed: how many trucks, which route, which day, and who your field contact is."
* *Instead of:* "If your men stop us again, we will report it to our headquarters and the donors."
* *Try:* "If something goes wrong on the day, let us agree to pause, call each other and meet, with no reprisals on either side."
