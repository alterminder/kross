# CCHN Tool 9: Analyzing the Position of the Counterpart

## What it is for

Before you plan tactics, understand the counterpart's position in a holistic, non-judgmental way (p. 199). The goal is not to agree with it. The goal is to see the reasoning and motives behind it, especially loss, fear and grievances, which drive many frontline positions.

The analysis shows where the parties converge and diverge. It also tells you whether to treat the negotiation as technical, professional or political, and whether to politicize or depoliticize it (p. 201).

## The method

**Three questions (pp. 199-200)**

* **WHAT** is the position, explicit or implicit? What does the other side want, and on what terms?
* **HOW** did they get there? The reasoning may be logical (a priori, a fortiori, a contrario), professional (legality, accountability, national security) or a military code (military necessity, proportionality). Is there a consensus around it?
* **WHY** do they hold it? Look at values, identity, social norms, emotions (hope, anger, fear, frustration) and deep-rooted needs such as security, recognition or sovereignty.

**The iceberg (Figure 3, p. 202).** The position is the visible tip. Tactical reasoning lies below it, and motives, values and identity lie deepest. You read a counterpart top-down, from what they say. The deeper you go, the more speculative your reading becomes (p. 205).

**Step 1: Gather information and test clarity and authority (pp. 209-213).** Use the three-pronged test:
1. **Authority.** How much authority does this interlocutor have, and how explicit is the representation? A minister or commander carries weight. An informal, undocumented message carries less, and a self-appointed unknown agent probably has little. Do not dismiss weak signals, though. They may be a way to pass a message without formalizing it (p. 210).
2. **Clarity.** A distinct proposal or a plain yes or no is probably authoritative. A convoluted position probably comes from a weaker source or was distorted on the way (p. 210).
3. **Predictability of timing, location and format.** Authoritative messages come through the expected channel. A foreign minister sends a Note Verbale, not a social media post. Reply in the same form and timing (p. 210).

The test covers verbal and non-verbal signals. In the manual's cross-line convoy case (pp. 210-213), a friendly corporal, a silent wave, an intelligence officer's warning and a radio message all conflict. A positive answer can mask an intent to attack, and humanitarians have died misreading such signals. Seek clarification whenever needed (p. 213).

**Step 2: Identify the tactical reasoning (pp. 213-215).** Look for logic a third party could follow, without accepting its premise or outcome. Counterparts seldom state their reasoning. It often comes out in informal conversation, through support-team members, local staff or acquaintances (pp. 203-204).

**Step 3: Identify values and motives (pp. 215-216).** These are values, identity and cultural norms that the counterpart often cannot control. An agreement is unlikely unless some of them are respected, explicitly or implicitly. Do not try to argue them away. They are more emotional than logical (p. 205).

**Worksheets.** The Negotiator Handbook has a WHAT/HOW/WHY worksheet (p. 46). The Facilitator Handbook checklist (Annex 6.4, p. 67) adds useful prompts: the context of the communication, the premises of the reasoning, and the risk to the agency of engaging on values.

## At the table

* Separate the explicit position from the implicit one. In the manual's detained-staff case, the implicit position was that staff would be released only once guarantees were given (p. 207).
* Check who decided and through which channel before you act: *"Thank you. Before we move, can the commander's office confirm the authorization the usual way?"*
* Draw out reasoning with open questions: *"How was this decision reached? What concern does it address for you?"*
* Approach values gently and later in the conversation: *"What would people here expect of you in this situation?"*
* Note what is not being said openly (NH p. 43), and check it later through your team.

## What to avoid

* Focusing too early on points of divergence (p. 199).
* Judging reasoning before you understand it. Even logic that leads to a war crime must be understood (pp. 214-215).
* Treating an ambiguous or low-authority signal as a decision, or ignoring it completely.
* Overconfidence. The analysis is deductive, and it gets more speculative at each lower layer. Do it as a team whose members challenge each other (p. 216). Skipping it can cost the negotiation dearly (p. 205).

## Key lines from the manual

> "The goal is to avoid focusing too early on the points of divergence and try to elucidate the counterpart's inner reasoning and inner motives" (p. 199)

> "A minimum of clarity and authority must be recognized before moving forward with the analysis" (p. 203)

> "The point here is not to agree with the logic but to understand the argument from the rational perspective of the counterpart." (p. 214)

> "A negotiation at the values level is most sensitive and involves a high level of risk" (p. 216)

> "It is important therefore to diversify the sources of information and remain cautious in their interpretation." (p. 217)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "Your policy is illegal under international humanitarian law."
* *Try:* "Help me understand how you see the risk. What is your main concern about a clinic near the front line?"

* *Instead of:* (a junior soldier says "you can go") "Great, we're leaving now."
* *Try:* "Thank you. Could your commanding officer confirm this through the usual channel before the convoy moves?"

## How KROSS uses it

* Each counterpart has an `iceberg` with three parts:
  * `position.stated` and `position.implicit` (WHAT).
  * `tactical_reasoning` (HOW), typed as legal, professional, logical, military or political.
  * `motives_values` (WHY).
* `hidden_interests` come out in iceberg order, unlocked by `asks_good_question`, `identifies_interest` and trust. Reasoning comes first; motives and values need higher trust. This mirrors the manual: reasoning is seldom stated, and values are the most sensitive layer.
* `counterparts[].authority` lists what the interlocutor decides and what they must consult on. Use it to judge whether a "yes" really carries authority.
* In the debrief, compare your WHAT/HOW/WHY guesses with the scenario iceberg. Which layers were confirmed, and which stayed speculation?
