# CCHN Tool 16: External Communication Around the Negotiation Process

## What it is for
Communication outside the room can make or break the relationship inside it. Statements from headquarters, the country office or the communications department "may have severe consequences on the trust and expectations of the parties" (p. 332). Tool 16 helps the mandator, support team and negotiator protect the confidentiality of the process and plan who says what, to whom (NH p. 77). The aim is to "preserve the privileged relationships with the counterparts and stakeholders" while still answering outside questions (p. 338).

## The method

**Basic duties (p. 332)**
- Keep the negotiation process confidential, and monitor information about the situation as far as possible.
- Prepare information briefings as circumstances require, and give the country and negotiation teams public-communication resources.
- Keep the lead negotiator in control of communication on the process at the local level, "even coming from HQ". In today's media environment, local information can go global within hours.

**Step 1: one clear message, built from the organisation's iceberg (pp. 332-333; NH p. 77)**
The mandator makes sure the organisation has a clear public message about its activities and, if necessary, the negotiation. That message should rest on:
- **uncontested facts and convergent norms** from the Island of Agreement (Tool 2), so it supports the negotiator's tactical plan;
- **the organisation's own iceberg**: **WHY** it wants to operate here (principles, motives, values, needs); **HOW** it works (problems addressed, professional tools and methods, difficulties met); and **WHAT** its position is (offer of service and starting terms, "best-case scenario of an agreement").
The communications department must be integrated into the negotiation chain. In a tense negotiation "even the life of the frontline negotiator may be at risk" (p. 333). The HfA example sets out the message in three parts: core mission, how HfA works, and the negotiation process (pp. 334-336; NH template p. 79).

**Step 2: distribute responsibilities (pp. 336-337; NH p. 78)**
Roles are split among the frontline negotiator, the head of the local office, senior management and the Country Director, and the Communication Department at HQ. Messages "coming from any part of the organization are inherently part of the negotiation process" (p. 336).

**Step 3: match the response to each audience (p. 337; NH pp. 78, 80)**
For each audience (local, national, international, donors and other international actors, other stakeholders including staff families), rate the expected demand for information (low, mid, high) and agree a response. In the HfA example:
- the local team briefs local media weekly under the lead negotiator's guidance;
- the Country Director keeps links with media and government after consulting the lead negotiator;
- the HQ communications department keeps a reactive line, in consultation with the Country Director;
- senior management briefs donors;
- the Country Director briefs staff and the families of the detained staff.

**Clearance and reporting (pp. 336-337).** In the HfA case the mandator decides that the negotiator reports weekly or every two weeks. The mandator and team agree an external communication strategy and pro forma lines. All communications must be cleared by the negotiation team, so the negotiator "is not surprised by any communications and has the opportunity to inform counterparts in advance."

## At the table
- Agree what stays private: "Can we agree that neither of us comments publicly on these talks while they continue?" In the FM's HfA tier 1 messages, keeping the discussion confidential is listed as a quick win (p. 306).
- Warn before anything goes out: "Our office will publish a short update on the hospital tomorrow. I wanted you to see the wording first."
- If the counterpart has read something unexpected, bring the conversation back to the table: "I'd like you to hear our position directly from me. What we are asking for is safe access for our staff and for emergency care to resume."
- Use the same WHY / HOW / WHAT in the room as in public, so the two never contradict each other.

## What to avoid
- HQ, the country office or the press office speaking about the process without the negotiation team's clearance. Surprise statements damage trust and expectations (p. 332).
- Treating the communications department as outside the negotiation (p. 333).
- Public messages built on contested facts or divergent norms instead of what supports the negotiator's tactical plan (p. 333).
- Sharing details of the talks, or of the confidential terms of your mandate, with outside audiences.

## Key lines from the manual
> "It is vital that the lead negotiator remain in control of the communication on the negotiation process at the local level, even coming from HQ" (p. 332)

> "every bit of local information may go global in a matter of hours." (p. 332)

> "messages coming from any part of the organization are inherently part of the negotiation process." (NH p. 78)

## Try instead
* *Instead of:* "If we don't get access today, our headquarters will take this to the media."
* *Try:* "I'd prefer to keep this between us while we work on it. What would you need from us so the team can get back to the hospital?"
* *Instead of:* "Our donors are already asking why you are holding our staff."
* *Try:* "Our people and their families are worried, and so are we. Let's agree on what we can each tell them about the steps we're taking together."

## How KROSS uses it
- **Moves**: `discloses_confidential` covers revealing confidential terms or talk details, and raises the `confidentiality` flag. A threat to "go to the media" is scored as `threatens`, which draws on CCHN Tool 8, not on this tool.
- **Dials**: unannounced outside statements cost `trust`. Coordinated, pre-cleared messages protect it.
- **trainee_brief.mandate** and **stakeholders**: who the trainee may speak for, and which outside audiences (donors, families, HQ) the scenario includes.
