# CCHN Tool 5: Drawing the Pathway of a Normative Negotiation

## What it is for

Building on the Island of Agreements, decide whether the negotiation is mainly about contested facts or divergent norms. Each needs its own pathway (pp. 137-139).

- **The counterpart contests the facts** ("There is no famine here"). This is a factual negotiation. Build on the norms you share, then support the presentation of evidence.
- **The counterpart contests the norm** ("You have no right to be here"). This is a normative negotiation. Build on the facts you share, then work toward consensus on the norm.
- **Both are contested.** The manual calls this a sign of "the absence of grounds to negotiate". Turn to the network of influence instead (p. 139).

Norms are harder than facts: "Beliefs do not need factual evidence" to survive (p. 140).

## The method

**1. Factual violation or normative divergence?** The manual's example is a commander who recruited 250 children (pp. 142-144).
- *Necessity* ("the siege forced us"). The norm is not challenged, only its application. This is a **factual** negotiation about which circumstances could justify an exception (p. 143).
- *Alternative local norm* ("at puberty, boys serve as a rite of passage"). This is a true **normative** negotiation: international norm N against local norm N′. The aim is better compliance with N "without prompting an aggravation of the violation of the local norm N′" (p. 143).
- N′ only counts as a norm if a real community believes in it. That means more than the violators themselves: elders, religious scholars, parliamentarians (p. 144).

**2. The four verification steps** (pp. 145-147):
1. **Verify that the international norm exists.** Agencies often treat vague policy positions as law, such as a "right of humanitarian access" or staff immunity from local laws. Be able to explain the norm simply.
2. **Verify the violation.** Check the evidence, recurrence and scale. Scattered incidents may not justify raising it. A normative negotiation needs violations that are "systematic and intended". If the counterpart simply did not know the norm, start by explaining it (p. 146).
3. **Verify that an alternative norm exists.** Look into the community's culture and local law. A practice is not "normal" just because it happened before.
4. **Map the competing norms and draw pathways of convergence.** Use two axes. X is the norm's formal character: **Legal vs. Social**. Y is its origin: **Global vs. Local**. Place both norms on the map.

**3. Four options, in order of feasibility** (pp. 148-149; NH template, pp. 26-27):
1. **Local and social.** Point to other local customs that sit closer to the international norm. Example: children stay in school or on the farm until 16, and girls are not recruited.
2. **Local and legal.** Point to national law, such as a national minimum recruitment age of 16.
3. **Global and social.** Discuss the moral character of the practice on the basis of a universal belief. Examples: the risk of sexual exploitation, or professional military norms.
4. **Global and legal.** Ask for compliance with international law, such as liability before the ICC. This is "probably the least likely option".

**4. Logical arguments** (pp. 149-150). Challenge the *logic* of the local norm, not its legitimacy:
- **A fortiori:** if women are exempt because they are vulnerable, the more vulnerable children should be too.
- **A contrario:** if the militia is made up of the strongest, the weakest (children) should be exempt.
- **A priori:** children depend on others' care, so they would be a burden on the militia.

Then present the other norms as "more solid", not as superior or more legitimate (p. 150).

## At the table

- Test whether N′ has a community behind it: "Who decides when a boy is ready to serve? Would the elders say the same?"
- Start with option 1: "Many families here keep their children on the farm until 16. How does that fit with taking them at 12?"
- Then option 2: "The national law your forces operate under sets 16 as the minimum. How is that applied in this district?"
- Use logic: "Your rules already spare women because they are vulnerable. Does the same reasoning not apply even more to the children?"

## What to avoid

- Treating a necessity argument as a clash of norms "in the absence of a community of belief" behind it (p. 151).
- Presenting a policy position as binding law (p. 145).
- Opening with option 4. It is the least likely to work.
- Attacking the legitimacy of the local norm instead of questioning its logic.
- Avoiding the subject altogether. Step back from your own beliefs (p. 144), but do not "shy away" from a genuine conflict of norms (p. 151).

## Key lines from the manual

> "Norms are parts of the 'DNA' of the parties to the negotiation and cannot simply be 'reconciled' without the buy-in of the original constituencies." (p. 137)

> "the law is probably more conservative than the understanding of it by the humanitarian agencies." (p. 145)

> "The words of the counterpart are clearly not sufficient." (p. 146)

> "The point is not to put into question the legitimacy of the local norm but rather to challenge its logic" (p. 149)

## Try instead

* *Instead of:* "Under international law, we have the right to access these people."
* *Try:* "We are here to deliver food to the families in the camp. Let us look together at how that can work in your area."
* *Instead of:* "Recruiting children is a war crime and your commanders will answer for it in The Hague."
* *Try:* "Your community already keeps girls out of the fighting. What would the same custom say about the youngest boys?"

## How KROSS uses it

- **negotiation.type** (`factual` or `normative`) records which pathway the scenario calls for. **island_of_agreements** (contested facts, divergent norms) is where the diagnosis starts.
- **overclaims_norm** is the Step 1 failure: asserting a norm that does not exist or does not bind here. It raises **rigidity** and lowers **trust**.
- **argues_principles_only** covers jumping straight to global legal or moral obligations.
- **asks_good_question** covers Steps 2-3: asking for evidence, about who holds the local norm, and about national law. Such questions can unlock **counterparts[].hidden_interests**. The local norm usually sits in **counterparts[].iceberg.motives_values**.
